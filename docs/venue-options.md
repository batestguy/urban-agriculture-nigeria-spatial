## Read this first: what I could and could not verify

**I could not verify Scopus indexing.** The Scopus source list and SCImago are both behind
JavaScript/login walls that this environment cannot pass. Any claim below about Scopus status
comes from a search summary, and search summaries have already been wrong three times in this
project — once supplying a DOI that resolved to a different paper entirely.

**You can check a journal's Scopus status in about thirty seconds**, and you should, before
submitting anywhere:

1. Go to **https://www.scopus.com/sources**
2. Search the journal title or ISSN
3. If it appears with **active coverage to the present**, it is indexed

Alternatively, download the monthly **Scopus source list** from
https://www.elsevier.com/products/scopus/content and filter by title or ISSN. That file is the
authority; nothing else is.

**What I did verify**, from the DOAJ API (a live registry, not a summary): publisher, country,
ISSN, article processing charge, licence, peer-review model, and typical time to publication.
Every field below comes from that run and is reproducible with
`python scripts/find_journals.py`.

One caveat on DOAJ's APC field: it records what journals declare, and can be stale or unset.
Confirm fees on the journal's own page before submitting.

## On "journals that permit AI use"

This framing needs correcting, because it is not the constraint you think it is.

**Almost every reputable journal already permits AI-assisted writing.** Roughly 70% of journals
now carry an AI policy, and the near-universal form is *permit with disclosure*. The Committee on
Publication Ethics position — followed by the large majority of publishers — is:

> AI tools cannot be listed as authors, because they cannot take responsibility for the work,
> manage copyright, or be held accountable. Authors remain fully responsible for all content,
> including anything AI-assisted, and **must disclose** AI use.

So the question is not *which journal will allow it*. It is:

1. **Does this journal require a disclosure statement, and in what form?** (Nearly all do.)
2. **Does it restrict AI-generated figures or images?** (Some do — this matters here, because
   every figure in the manuscript was generated by code we wrote, not by a generative image
   model. That is a point in your favour and belongs in the disclosure.)
3. **Does it use AI-detection screening, and how does it treat false positives?** (A few do;
   treatment varies.)

**A minority of journals ban AI-generated text outright.** These are the ones to identify and
avoid, not because the policy is unreasonable but because compliance would be impossible to
demonstrate. Check the author guidelines for the word "generative" before submitting.

### What to disclose for this manuscript

Stating it plainly is safer than being vague. Something like:

> **Declaration of generative AI use.** The author used an AI coding assistant during the
> preparation of this work. It assisted with implementing and debugging the analysis pipeline,
> running verification checks, and drafting and editing the manuscript text. All research
> design, data collection, analytical decisions and interpretation are the author's own. Every
> numerical result was independently re-derived from the frozen data by committed verification
> scripts, and every cited reference was resolved against the Crossref registry. The author
> takes full responsibility for the content.

That is accurate, it is specific, and it pre-empts the obvious question. Journal editors have
seen hundreds of these. Undisclosed use is the actual risk; disclosed use is routine.

## Candidate venues

Ordered by how well they fit, not by prestige.

### 1. African Journal on Land Policy and Geospatial Sciences

- **Publisher:** EL-AYACHI (Morocco) · **e-ISSN:** 2657-2664
- **APC:** none (DOAJ) · **Licence:** CC BY-NC-SA · **Peer review:** single-anonymous
- **Typical time:** ~12 weeks · **Open access since:** 2018
- **Why it fits:** explicitly African, explicitly geospatial, explicitly land policy. The
  manuscript's subject matter — where cultivation concentrates, and what that means for land
  allocation — sits exactly in its scope. Its readership includes the planning practitioners
  the paper addresses.
- **Scopus:** widely reported as indexed; **verify yourself**.

### 2. Town and Regional Planning

- **Publisher:** University of the Free State (South Africa) · **ISSN:** 1012-280X / 2415-0495
- **APC:** none (DOAJ) · **Licence:** CC BY · **Typical time:** ~18 weeks
- **Why it fits:** a Southern African planning journal with an established record on urban
  agriculture in the region. Good regional readership.
- **Caveat:** I could not cite to this journal earlier in the project because its Crossref
  metadata is unreliable — author records are missing and one DOI resolved to an unrelated
  paper. That says nothing about its editorial quality, but check the current author
  guidelines carefully.
- **Scopus:** reported as indexed; **verify yourself**.

### 3. TeMA: Journal of Land Use, Mobility and Environment

- **Publisher:** Università di Napoli Federico II (Italy) · **ISSN:** 1970-9889 / 1970-9870
- **APC:** none (DOAJ) · **Licence:** CC BY · **Typical time:** ~10 weeks
- **Why it fits:** land use and urban environment, with a quantitative/spatial orientation.
  Less Africa-specific than 1 and 2, which is a weakness for this paper's framing.
- **Scopus:** reported as indexed; **verify yourself**.

### 4. Frontiers of Urban and Rural Planning

- **Publisher:** Springer · **ISSN:** 2731-6661
- **APC:** none (DOAJ) · **Licence:** CC BY · **Typical time:** ~25 weeks · **Since 2023**
- **Why it fits:** directly on the urban–rural continuum, which is the manuscript's frame.
  Springer infrastructure, and a newer journal still building its reputation.
- **Scopus:** a 2023 launch is unlikely to have Scopus coverage yet, or to have it fully.
  **Check carefully** — this is the one most likely to fail that test.

### 5. Journal of Remote Sensing (AAAS)

- **Publisher:** American Association for the Advancement of Science · **ISSN:** 2694-1589
- **APC:** none (DOAJ) · **Licence:** CC BY · **Typical time:** ~16 weeks
- **Why it fits:** strong venue with no APC, but the paper is not a remote-sensing methods
  paper. It would need reframing around the volunteered-geographic-information contribution.
- **Scopus:** likely indexed; **verify**.

## A caution about the question itself

The question "which journal will agree to publish this" has a benign reading and a risky one.
The benign reading is *which venues are a good fit*. The risky one leads toward journals whose
acceptance criterion is payment rather than review.

Two signals that a journal returned by a keyword search is not worth submitting to:

- It appears in DOAJ but publishes across wildly unrelated subjects (one result from my search
  covered agriculture, engineering and business in the same title and charges nothing).
- Its publisher is a for-profit entity with no institutional or society backing, a very short
  time to publication (~4–6 weeks), and no preservation arrangement.

DOAJ listing is **not** a quality signal. It means the journal is open access and honest about
its terms; it does not mean it reviews rigorously. Three entries my search returned are, on
those indicators alone, ones I would not recommend.

**The manuscript is now good enough to survive real peer review**, which is the point of having
written it this way. That is worth more than a fast acceptance. The verification work — 175
automated checks, every citation resolved against Crossref, the withdrawn hypothesis stated as
withdrawn — is precisely the material a good reviewer probes, and it holds up.

## What remains before submission

**Manuscript-level**

1. **Choose a venue** and reformat to its house style (reference style, section order, abstract
   length, figure resolution).
2. **Add the AI declaration** from the template above.
3. **Confirm the author list** and corresponding-author details.
4. **Check the figure count** against the venue's limit. The manuscript has five; some journals
   cap at four for a research article.

**Content-level, disclosed in the manuscript already**

5. The `farmland`-is-not-`allotments` distinction is stated in the Limitations. A reviewer at a
   planning journal may push on it. The honest answer is in the paper.
6. The hurdle-model question: a methods-minded reviewer may argue a zero-inflated specification
   is preferable to a single-equation negative binomial for a 95%-zero outcome. The manuscript
   reports the hurdle's identification failure rather than hiding it, which is the right
   starting position for that conversation.
7. H2 is withdrawn. If a reviewer asks why it was included at all, the answer is that reporting
   a withdrawn hypothesis is more useful than omitting it — and the manuscript says so.

**Not required, but improves the paper**

8. Integrating CHIRPS rainfall at LGA resolution would convert a withdrawn hypothesis into a
   testable one (`funk2015` is already cited). This is the single largest scientific gain
   available, and it is a tractable piece of work.
